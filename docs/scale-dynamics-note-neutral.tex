\section{Purpose and summary of conclusions}
% ============================================================

The previous investigation
(\code{docs/rblapsum-kappa-stability-neutral.tex})
introduced the geometric quantity

\[
\chigeo
=
\frac{b}{2T\delta}
\]

as an empirical indicator of instability.

The purpose of the present investigation is to test whether
\(\chigeo\) is a fundamental control parameter or only a useful proxy,
and to identify the mechanism that produces the observed
score-inflation bursts.

Four hypotheses are considered:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item \textbf{H1:}
\(\chigeo\) is an empirical proxy rather than a fundamental order parameter.

\item \textbf{H2:}
the dimensionless surrogate gain

\[
\Pisurr
=
\left\|L_\kappa D_z\right\|_F^2
\]

is a more appropriate measure of the strength of the support-gradient
mechanism.

\item \textbf{H3:}
large surrogate gain destabilizes the model by coupling to the score-scale
degree of freedom.

\item \textbf{H4:}
the collapse of the effective kernel population,
\(\neff\to1\), is primarily a late-stage feature of the instability rather
than its initial cause.

\end{itemize}

The main results are as follows.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item \textbf{H1 is supported.}
A score-unit reparameterization leaves the forward computation and training
dynamics unchanged while changing \(\chigeo\) by an arbitrary multiplicative
factor. In addition, within a fixed \((K,T)\) row, \(\chigeo\) has essentially
no prospective predictive power for future bursts
(AUC approximately \(0.46\)).
Therefore a universal numerical threshold in \(\chigeo\) cannot be
fundamental.

\item \textbf{H2 is supported as a statement about surrogate gain.}
The quantity

\[
\Pisurr
=
\left\|L_\kappa D_z\right\|_F^2
\]

is dimensionless and invariant under the score-unit reparameterization.
Across the measured states it predicts the realized gradient gain with rank
correlation \(0.97\), compared with \(0.52\) for \(\chigeo\).
The approximation

\[
\Pisurr
\approx
\frac{b^2}{4T\delta}
\]

is reasonably accurate in healthy, locally dense boundary regions, but can
fail badly when the score geometry becomes degenerate.

\item \textbf{H3 is supported, but the dominant mechanism is not a positive
first-order radial force.}
At the dangerous states, the signed task and surrogate contributions to the
score scale are generally restoring.
The destabilizing contribution is instead a second-order increase in score
scale caused by large parameter updates.
We refer to this contribution as \emph{heating}.
It is even under reversal of the gradient direction and scales approximately
quadratically with the learning-rate multiplier.
At the most dangerous states it is largely deterministic rather than a
consequence of minibatch variance.
Instability begins when this heating becomes comparable to or larger than the
restoring signed drift.

\item \textbf{H4 is supported with an important qualification.}
In the strict one-feature limit,
\(\neff=1\), the kappa-corrected surrogate vanishes:
the measured \(\Pisurr\) is exactly zero.
However, neighboring blocks with
\(\neff\approx2\) and very large activations can retain enormous surrogate
gain and continue to increase the score scale.
Post-collapse growth stops when the support gradient is disabled, while
broadening the kernel after collapse can make the instability substantially
worse.

\item \textbf{The resulting controller picture has two relevant variables.}
The effective kernel population \(\neff\) provides the strongest measured
within-row early warning of a burst, while \(\Pisurr\) measures the strength
of the surrogate coupling.
After a block has already entered the collapsed regime, the appropriate
emergency intervention is to suppress the support gradient rather than to
increase \(T\).

\end{itemize}

\section{Definitions}
% ============================================================

All gate quantities below are computed per layer from the candidate set,
consisting of the top \(K+J\) features by score.

\subsection*{Scores, boundary, and kernel}

The pre-gate feature value is \(z_i\), and the score used for ranking is

\[
s_i=|z_i|.
\]

The rank boundary is

\[
b
=
\max\left(b_0,s_{(K+1)}\right),
\]

where \(s_{(K+1)}\) is the largest inactive score.

The exponential kernel is

\[
\kappa_i
=
\frac{e^{-|s_i-b|/T}}{2T},
\qquad
Z=\sum_i\kappa_i.
\]

The effective kernel population is

\[
\neff
=
\frac{Z^2}{\sum_i\kappa_i^2}.
\]

A local rank-spacing estimate is

\[
\delta
=
\frac{s_{(K-3)}-s_{(K+5)}}{8}.
\]

Small \(\delta\) corresponds to a locally dense score distribution near the
support boundary.

\subsection*{Gradient chain}

It is useful to make the backward path explicit.
Let \(p_i\) denote the surrogate gate variable and let the bottleneck output be

\[
y_i=p_i z_i.
\]

The gradient arriving from the rest of the network is

\[
u_i
=
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_i}.
\]

At fixed \(z_i\),

\[
\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial p_i}
=
z_i,
\]

so

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial p}
=
D_z u,
\qquad
D_z=\operatorname{diag}(z).
\]

For the kappa-corrected surrogate, define

\[
D_\kappa
=
\operatorname{diag}(\kappa)
\]

and

\[
L_\kappa
=
D_\kappa
-
\frac{\kappa\kappa^\top}{Z}.
\]

The surrogate score-to-gate Jacobian is represented by \(L_\kappa\).
The support-gradient contribution with respect to the scores is therefore

\[
g_s
=
L_\kappa D_z u.
\]

Equivalently, with

\[
w_i=-u_i z_i,
\]

one may write

\[
g_s=-L_\kappa w,
\]

up to the sign convention used for the reported update direction.

Because

\[
L_\kappa \mathbf 1=0,
\]

the kappa correction removes the common score-translation mode from the
surrogate gradient.

\subsection*{Exact surrogate gain}

The dimensionless gain of the surrogate Jacobian is defined as

\[
\boxed{
\Pisurr
=
\left\|L_\kappa D_z\right\|_F^2.
}
\]

Using the diagonal-minus-rank-one form of \(L_\kappa\), this can be computed
without constructing a dense matrix:

\[
\Pisurr
=
\sum_j
z_j^2\kappa_j^2
\left[
\left(1-\frac{\kappa_j}{Z}\right)^2
+
\frac{
\sum_i\kappa_i^2-\kappa_j^2
}{
Z^2
}
\right].
\]

The quantity \(\kappa_j z_j\) is dimensionless, so \(\Pisurr\) is also
dimensionless.

By contrast,

\[
\chigeo
=
\frac{b}{2T\delta}
\]

has units of inverse score.

For a locally dense and approximately uniform score distribution near the
boundary,

\[
\Pisurr
\approx
\frac{b^2}{4T\delta}.
\]

This approximation is tested below rather than assumed to hold generally.

The realized empirical gain is measured as

\[
G_{\mathrm{emp}}
=
\frac{\|g_s\|^2}{\|u\|^2}
\]

over the candidate features.

\subsection*{Score-scale statistics}

On a fixed evaluation batch, three scale statistics are monitored:

\[
R_{\mathrm{all}}
=
\operatorname{RMS}_i(s_i),
\]

the mean top-\(K\) score \(R_K\), and the boundary \(b\).

The logarithmic scale coordinate is

\[
x=\log R.
\]

Using \(\log R\) makes multiplicative changes in score scale additive.

\subsection*{One-step drift and heating decomposition}

Counterfactual optimizer steps are evaluated from the same saved model and
optimizer state.

The branches are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item \emph{null}: zero current gradient;
\item \emph{task}: support-surrogate gradient disabled;
\item \emph{full}: ordinary task gradient plus support-surrogate gradient;
\item the corresponding antithetic branches in which the current gradient is
reversed.

\end{itemize}

The saved Adam-type optimizer state is retained in every branch, so previously
accumulated momentum and second moments are replayed identically.

For a gradient \(G\), define the signed, or odd, component of the one-step
scale change as

\[
\mu(G)
=
\frac12
\mathbb E
\left[
\Delta x(+G)-\Delta x(-G)
\right].
\]

This isolates the part that changes sign when the current gradient direction
is reversed.

The support contribution to the signed drift is obtained from

\[
\mu_{\mathrm{supp}}
=
\mu_{\mathrm{full}}
-
\mu_{\mathrm{task}}.
\]

The even component relative to the null branch measures the
sign-independent change in scale.
At second order, parameter displacements contribute positively to squared
activation norms even when their first-order radial contribution changes
sign.
This even contribution is referred to below as \emph{heating}.

The raw gradients themselves satisfy the exact decomposition

\[
G_{\mathrm{full}}
=
G_{\mathrm{task}}
+
G_{\mathrm{supp}},
\]

which was verified using identical random-number states and in a model with
no stochastic layers.
Optimizer updates are not additive in the same way, because the Adam-type
update is nonlinear in the current gradient and optimizer state.
For this reason the optimizer branches are measured separately.


% ============================================================
\section{Experiments performed}
% ============================================================

The investigation used the following experiments.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]

\item \textbf{Checkpoint library.}
Thirteen model states were selected to cover several regimes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item calm \(T=2\) states;
\item stable \(K=32,T=1\) states;
\item a marginal surviving \(K=32,J=224,T=0.5\) run around a recoverable
burst, using checkpoints at steps 2000, 4000, and 6000;
\item marginal checkpoints from runs that later collapsed,
including \(K=32,J=480,T=0.5\) and \(K=64,J=448,T=1\);
\item a post-collapse \(K=32,J=480,T=0.1\) state.

\end{itemize}

All checkpoints include the full optimizer state.

The state labels describe the eventual fate of the corresponding run.
Except for the explicitly post-collapse checkpoint, the model is still in a
healthy state at the time of measurement.
For example, the \(K=64,T=1\) ``pre-collapse'' checkpoint is taken at step
2000, while the original run destabilizes at approximately step 3940.

\item \textbf{One-step drift and heating.}
For each state, 16 batches were evaluated.
Five counterfactual optimizer branches were measured per batch, and the
per-layer change in \(\log R\) was evaluated on a fixed evaluation batch.

\item \textbf{Surrogate gain and radial structure.}
For each state and layer, the analysis measured

\[
\Pisurr,\qquad
\frac{b^2}{4T\delta},\qquad
\chigeo,\qquad
\neff,\qquad
G_{\mathrm{emp}},
\]

as well as the support radial projection

\[
r_{\mathrm{supp}}
=
-\frac{s^\top g_s^{(z)}}{s^\top s},
\]

the quantity

\[
(s-b\mathbf1)^\top L_\kappa w,
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Cov}_q(s-b,w).
\]

A permutation control was also performed using 48 random shuffles of \(w\)
among candidates for each token.

\item \textbf{Scale-response curves.}
A function-preserving gate-level intervention rescales

\[
z\rightarrow\alpha z
\]

while dividing the gate output by \(\alpha\).
This keeps the forward function and ordinary task-gradient path unchanged,
while the support surrogate sees scores multiplied by \(\alpha\) at fixed
temperature \(T\).

The intervention was unit-tested and verified numerically.
Drift was measured for

\[
\alpha
\in
\{0.5,0.75,1,1.25,1.5,2,3\}
\]

at four reference states.

\item \textbf{Score-unit reparameterization.}
The transformation

\[
z\rightarrow cz,
\qquad
s\rightarrow cs,
\qquad
b\rightarrow cb,
\qquad
T\rightarrow cT,
\]

together with the corresponding output compensation, should leave the gate
and parameter gradients invariant.

Under this transformation,

\[
\chigeo
\rightarrow
\frac{\chigeo}{c}.
\]

This provides a direct test of whether a numerical threshold in
\(\chigeo\) can be fundamental.

\item \textbf{Learning-rate and batch-size tests.}
Learning-rate multipliers

\[
\{0.5,1,2\}
\]

and a half-batch condition were evaluated at three representative states in
order to distinguish first-order signed drift from second-order or stochastic
effects.

\item \textbf{Counterfactual continuations.}
Longer continuations were run from a marginal \(K=96,T=1\) state and from the
post-collapse \(T=0.1\) state.

The interventions included:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item normal continuation with three random seeds;
\item disabling the support gradient from the start;
\item disabling the support gradient after a boundary-inflation trigger;
\item increasing \(T\) by a factor of 3 in the marginal state;
\item increasing \(T\) by a factor of 30 in the post-collapse state;
\item applying the temperature intervention either immediately or only after
the trigger.

\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Prospective comparison of candidate order parameters.}
A dataset of 148 checkpoint states from 18 earlier fixed-\(T\) runs was used
to test whether a quantity measured at step \(r\) predicts a boundary burst
during

\[
(r,r+H].
\]

Predictions were evaluated both after pooling all configurations and within
fixed \((K,T)\) rows.

\end{enumerate}


% ============================================================
\section{Results 1: reparameterization test (H1)}
% ============================================================

Consider the gate-level transformation

\[
(s,z,b,T)
\rightarrow
(cs,cz,cb,cT).
\]

With the corresponding output compensation, this transformation leaves the
forward computation and parameter gradients invariant.

This invariance is exact in the float32 single-gate unit tests.
It is also reproduced at full checkpoints.

For

\[
c=0.5
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
c=2,
\]

which are exactly representable binary rescalings, the training loss is
unchanged to all reported digits.
The largest relative parameter-gradient difference is approximately

\[
2\times10^{-3}
\text{--}
6\times10^{-3},
\]

consistent with differences in bf16 accumulation order.

For \(c=3\), which is not an exact power-of-two scaling, some nearly tied
scores change ordering in bf16.
This changes candidate membership and produces a loss shift of order

\[
10^{-3}.
\]

This effect is attributed to finite-precision ranking rather than to a
violation of the underlying reparameterization symmetry.

Under exactly the same transformation,

\[
\chigeo
\rightarrow
\frac{\chigeo}{c}.
\]

For example, in layer 4 the measured values are

\[
54.1
\quad(c=1),
\]

\[
27.0
\quad(c=2),
\]

and

\[
18.1
\quad(c=3).
\]

In contrast, \(\Pisurr\) is invariant.

Therefore functionally equivalent gate parameterizations can have different
values of \(\chigeo\).
This rules out a universal numerical instability threshold based directly on
\(\chigeo\).

The prospective analysis in Section~\ref{sec:prospective} gives a second,
independent limitation: within a fixed \((K,T)\) row,
\(\chigeo\) has approximately chance-level predictive performance.

\paragraph{H1 conclusion.}
The measurements support interpreting \(\chigeo\) as an empirical
configuration-level proxy rather than a fundamental order parameter.
It distinguishes some \((K,T)\) regimes because it co-varies with the local
kernel geometry, but it is neither reparameterization-invariant nor a useful
within-row temporal warning signal.

\section{Results 2: surrogate gain \texorpdfstring{$\Pi$}{Pi} (H2)}
% ============================================================

Across 96 layer states
(12 checkpoints \(\times\) 8 blocks, excluding the post-collapse state),
the exact quantity

\[
\Pisurr
=
\|L_\kappa D_z\|_F^2
\]

predicts the realized gradient gain

\[
G_{\mathrm{emp}}
=
\frac{\|g_s\|^2}{\|u\|^2}
\]

with rank correlation

\[
0.97.
\]

For comparison, the rank correlation with \(\chigeo\) is

\[
0.52.
\]

This strongly supports the interpretation of \(\Pisurr\) as the relevant
surrogate-Jacobian gain.

The dense-boundary approximation

\[
\Pisurr
\approx
\frac{b^2}{4T\delta}
\]

is typically accurate within a factor of approximately two in mid-depth
blocks of healthy states.

Its accuracy deteriorates as the local score distribution becomes
nonuniform or degenerate.
The discrepancy reaches approximately a factor of 10 during late inflation
and approximately \(10^4\) after collapse.

The approximation can also overestimate the gain by orders of magnitude in
individual blocks where several rank scores become nearly tied.
In that case,

\[
\delta\rightarrow0
\]

even though the exact kernel geometry and exact \(\Pisurr\) remain finite.

This is an important limitation of any order parameter based directly on the
single local-spacing statistic \(\delta\).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.9\textwidth]{figures/sdn\_Pi.png}
\caption{
Left: exact surrogate gain \(\Pisurr\) versus the realized gradient gain
\(\|g_s\|^2/\|u\|^2\).
Right: exact \(\Pisurr\) versus the dense-boundary approximation
\(b^2/(4T\delta)\).
Each point corresponds to one checkpoint and one block.
The checkpoint classes are calm (\(T=2\)), stable (\(K=32,T=1\)),
marginal (\(T=0.5\)), pre-collapse (\(K=64,T=1\)), and post-collapse
(\(T=0.1\)).
The class denotes the eventual fate of the run, not necessarily its condition
at the measured checkpoint.
In particular, the pre-collapse checkpoint is still healthy at step 2000 and
destabilizes approximately 1900 steps later.
Only the post-collapse points are measured after a collapse.
No averaging over blocks is performed.
Most checkpoints are taken at step 2000, with additional \(T=0.5\) states at
steps 4000 and 6000 and one calm and one stable checkpoint at step 10000.
Within each point, \(\Pisurr\) is averaged over tokens on the fixed evaluation
batch, while \(G_{\mathrm{emp}}\) is averaged over tokens on one training
microbatch.
}
\end{figure}

The earlier comparison between the \(K\)- and \(T\)-axes is approximately
reproduced by the dense-boundary formula.
At step 2000,

\[
\frac{
\Pi_{\mathrm{approx}}(K=64,T=1)
}{
\Pi_{\mathrm{approx}}(K=32,T=1)
}
=
1.66,
\]

within the previously predicted range \(1.6\)--\(2.25\).

However, for the exact gain the corresponding ratio is only

\[
1.09.
\]

The difference arises because the approximation replaces the actual kernel
and \(z\)-profile by a locally uniform-density model, whereas the exact
\(\Pisurr\) uses the full candidate geometry.

\paragraph{H2 conclusion.}
The data support \(\Pisurr\) as the appropriate measure of the instantaneous
gain of the support-surrogate Jacobian.
It is dimensionless, reparameterization-invariant, and closely predicts the
realized surrogate gradient gain.

This does \emph{not} by itself imply that a large \(\Pisurr\) is unstable.
Whether the injected gradient produces score-scale growth is a separate
dynamical question.

\section{Results 3: signed drift and second-order heating (H3)}
% ============================================================

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sdn\_decomposition.png}
\caption{
One-step change in \(\log R_{\mathrm{all}}\), decomposed into signed task
drift, signed support-surrogate drift, and the even heating contribution.
The signed terms are obtained from the antithetic gradient branches.
Results are pooled over blocks at thirteen checkpoint states.
}
\end{figure}

The decomposition gives three main results.

\subsection*{Signed surrogate drift is not the primary runaway term}

At the \(K=64,T=1\) checkpoint measured approximately 1940 steps before
collapse, the signed surrogate contribution is strongly restoring:

\[
\mu_{\mathrm{supp}}
\approx
-5.7\times10^{-2}
\]

per step when pooled over blocks, and approximately

\[
-0.10
\]

at the most extreme block.

The ordinary task contribution is also restoring:

\[
\mu_{\mathrm{task}}
\approx
-2.2\times10^{-2}.
\]

A similar pattern occurs during late inflation in the unstable \(T=0.5\)
run.

Thus the instability is not explained by a first-order support force that
directly pushes all scores outward.
At the measured dangerous states, the signed contribution tends to reduce
rather than increase the scale.

\subsection*{The dominant destabilizing contribution is second-order heating}

The even component of the update increases the activation scale independently
of the sign of the current gradient.

At the pre-collapse \(K=64,T=1\) state, this contribution reaches

\[
+9.2\times10^{-2}
\]

per step after pooling over blocks, and approximately

\[
+0.18
\]

at the most strongly affected block.

This exceeds the restoring signed drift.

At calm and stable checkpoints, the same heating term is approximately
30--300 times smaller.

The learning-rate sweep supports the interpretation as a second-order effect:
the signed odd terms scale approximately linearly with the learning-rate
multiplier, while the even contribution scales approximately quadratically.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sdn\_lr\_scaling.png}
\caption{\label{fig:lr}
Signed drift and heating as functions of the learning-rate multiplier at
three checkpoint states.
Reference linear and quadratic scalings are shown for comparison.
}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Dangerous-state heating is mostly deterministic}

At the pre-collapse state, halving the batch size changes the measured heating
only from approximately

\[
9.2\times10^{-2}
\]

to

\[
9.3\times10^{-2}.
\]

The variation across batches is also small.

Therefore the large heating at this checkpoint is not primarily caused by
minibatch-gradient noise.
It is better interpreted as deterministic second-order scale inflation caused
by a large structured optimizer step.

At the stable \(K=32,T=1\) state, the much smaller heating contribution
approximately doubles when the batch size is halved.
In that regime, stochastic gradient variance appears to make a substantial
contribution.

\subsection*{Scale-response curves}

The scale-response experiment isolates the signed support component.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sdn\_Falpha.png}
\caption{\label{fig:falpha}
Signed surrogate and task drift as functions of an imposed
function-preserving scale factor \(\alpha\) at four checkpoint states.
}
\end{figure}

At the calm \(K=64,T=2\) state, the support-surrogate drift crosses zero near

\[
\alpha\approx1.4.
\]

Below this point it tends to increase scale, while above it the sign becomes
restoring.
Within this gate-level intervention, the surrogate therefore behaves
approximately like a restoring force around a preferred scale.

At the stable \(K=32,T=1\) state, the signed surrogate contribution remains
positive throughout the measured range, but decreases in magnitude as
\(\alpha\) increases.

The task contribution is independent of \(\alpha\) by construction of the
intervention, and the measurements reproduce this property.
This provides an internal validation that the experiment isolates the
surrogate scale dependence as intended.

\subsection*{Rank structure of the signed force}

The permutation test asks whether the relationship between the task preference
\(w_i\) and feature rank matters, rather than only the overall magnitude of
\(w\).

In most measured states, the true signed radial component differs
significantly from the rank-shuffled null distribution, typically by

\[
|z\text{-score}|
\gtrsim
3\text{--}20.
\]

At the pre-collapse state, the alignment is outward relative to the shuffled
null,

\[
+19.8\sigma,
\]

whereas late calm states show inward alignment.

The absolute magnitude of this signed component is nevertheless small
compared with the heating term at the dangerous checkpoints.

The data therefore suggest that the rank structure of \(w\) determines the
signed scale response, which is often restoring, while the main
destabilization at high pressure is associated with the even second-order
term.

\paragraph{H3 conclusion.}
Large surrogate gain does not destabilize the model primarily through a
positive first-order radial force.

The measurements instead support the following local picture:

\[
\text{large support-gradient step}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{second-order score-scale heating},
\]

while the signed task and support components provide an opposing restoring
contribution.

A block becomes locally unstable when the heating contribution becomes
comparable to or larger than this restoration.

\section{Results 4: counterfactual interventions and collapse dynamics (H4)}
% ============================================================

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sdn\_interventions.png}
\caption{
Left: continuations from the marginal \(K=96,T=1\) state at step 2000,
comparing three normal seeds with support-off and \(T\times3\) interventions,
applied either immediately or after a boundary-inflation trigger.
Middle: continuations from the collapsed \(T=0.1\) state; solid lines show
scale and dotted lines show evaluation CE.
Right: one-step drift/heating decomposition along a densely sampled
\(K=96,T=0.75\) collapse trajectory, with checkpoints every 100 steps and
collapse near step 960.
}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Interventions from a marginal state}

Starting from the \(K=96,T=1\) checkpoint, three normal 1500-step
continuations were run.

All three show repeated score-scale excursions of approximately one to three
orders of magnitude.
Most excursions recover, but one of the three replicas reaches terminal
divergence.

When the support gradient is disabled, the trajectory becomes calm and the
score scale decreases.
This is consistent with the one-step measurements showing that the ordinary
task gradient is restoring at this state.

Increasing the temperature by a factor of three also suppresses the bursts
while keeping the scale approximately constant.
This branch obtains evaluation CE \(1.505\), compared with \(1.552\)--\(1.634\)
for the normal branches.

Both interventions remain effective when activated only at the first
boundary-inflation trigger, defined by the boundary exceeding three times its
rolling median.

These experiments show that, for this marginal checkpoint, the support
surrogate is necessary for the observed burst dynamics.

\subsection*{Interventions from an already collapsed state}

The post-collapse \(T=0.1\) checkpoint begins at a score scale of approximately

\[
10^6.
\]

Under normal continuation, the scale increases by another factor of
approximately \(25\) over 1200 steps, while CE remains near \(5.8\).

Increasing the temperature by a factor of 30 does not rescue this state.
Instead, the scale grows by approximately a factor of \(500\).

The reason is visible in the gate geometry.
Broadening the kernel repopulates candidate regions in a state where some
blocks already have extremely large surrogate gain,

\[
\Pisurr
\sim
10^{12}.
\]

At the same time, one block is exactly in the single-feature limit,

\[
\neff=1,
\qquad
\Pisurr=0.
\]

Thus the strict one-feature limit itself has zero corrected surrogate gain,
as predicted by

\[
L_\kappa\rightarrow0.
\]

Other near-degenerate blocks, however, still contain more than one
significantly weighted feature and have very large \(z\).
Their surrogate gain can therefore remain extremely large.

Disabling the support gradient stops the continued inflation.
The model then begins to recover, with evaluation CE decreasing from

\[
5.93
\]

to

\[
4.44
\]

over 1200 steps.

Therefore post-collapse inflation in this experiment is not maintained by
the task gradient alone.
It requires the remaining support-surrogate contribution.

\subsection*{Dense sampling of a collapse}

A separate \(K=96,T=0.75\) trajectory was sampled every 100 steps around its
collapse near step 960.

Approximately 200 steps before collapse, the one-step decomposition is still
moderate:

\[
\text{restoring drift}
\approx
-1.3\times10^{-2},
\]

\[
\text{heating}
\approx
+2.4\times10^{-3}.
\]

The heating is already approximately an order of magnitude larger than in
calm states, but restoration still dominates.

Around 60--100 steps before collapse, both contributions increase by roughly
two orders of magnitude:

\[
\text{surrogate signed drift}
\approx
-0.50,
\]

\[
\text{heating}
\approx
+0.37
\]

per step after pooling over blocks.

The model is therefore not experiencing a simple unopposed outward force.
Instead, very large restoring and destabilizing contributions coexist, and
their balance differs across layers.

After collapse, both measured terms become small again because much of the
kernel has effectively disappeared.

\paragraph{H4 conclusion.}
The measurements support treating \(\neff\to1\) as a late-stage consequence
of the instability rather than its initial trigger.

The exact \(\neff=1\) limit eliminates the corrected support gradient.
However, the approach to this limit is not harmless:
blocks with \(\neff\approx2\) and very large activations can retain extremely
large surrogate gain and continue to drive scale growth.

Thus two observations are simultaneously true:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]

\item the distributed character of the correction is lost as the kernel
population collapses;

\item the residual surrogate in neighboring near-degenerate blocks can remain
strongly destabilizing until it is disabled or the kernel becomes fully
inactive.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Results 6: drift, heating, and the escape picture}
% ============================================================

The learning-rate and batch-size experiments distinguish two contributions to
the scale dynamics.

The signed drift scales approximately linearly with the learning-rate
multiplier.

The even heating contribution scales approximately quadratically with the
learning-rate multiplier.

At the stable \(K=32,T=1\) state, halving the batch size approximately doubles
the heating contribution.
This indicates a substantial stochastic-gradient component in that regime.

At the pre-collapse \(K=64,T=1\) state, halving the batch leaves the heating
essentially unchanged.
The dominant heating there therefore appears to be deterministic
second-order overshoot from a large structured update.

The earlier observation of stochastic-looking score excursions is reproduced
directly by the marginal continuation experiment:
among three replicas from the same marginal state, one reaches terminal
collapse while two undergo bursts and recover.

These observations are consistent with a metastable escape picture, but the
available measurements do not yet support a quantitative reduction to a
single Kramers-like escape parameter.

For example, at the calm \(K=64,T=2\) state, the restoring scale-response
curve crosses zero near

\[
\alpha\approx1.4,
\]

with an estimated local slope

\[
\lambda
\approx
7\times10^{-3}
\]

per unit \(\log R\) per training step.

Comparable estimates of the restoring slope, effective excitation, and
distance to the collapse boundary are not yet available across enough
marginal configurations.

The qualitative picture supported by the current data is therefore:

\[
\text{restoring scale dynamics}
\;+\;
\text{second-order excitation}
\;+\;
\text{kernel-population collapse}.
\]

The task gradient generally supplies a restoring contribution.
The support surrogate can also provide a signed restoring contribution around
its scale-dependent operating point.

At the same time, large support-induced parameter updates generate
second-order heating.
If an excursion becomes sufficiently large, the effective kernel population
decreases and the distributed correction enters a near-degenerate regime.

This provides a mechanism for recoverable bursts as well as rare terminal
escapes without requiring a persistent positive first-order radial force.

\section{Proposed causal sequence for a burst}
% ============================================================

The following sequence summarizes the present interpretation.
Statements directly established by the measurements are marked
\textbf{(M)}; steps that remain interpretive are marked \textbf{(I)}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]

\item
In marginal configurations, the support surrogate has substantially larger
gain than in calm configurations:
the measured \(\Pisurr\) is approximately 3--10 times larger.
The resulting optimizer step produces second-order heating that is comparable
to, or exceeds, the restoring signed drift in the most affected blocks.
\textbf{(M)}

\item
This heating can produce temporary increases in score scale.
Both the task and signed support contributions generally oppose the scale
increase at the measured dangerous states.
Most observed excursions therefore recover.
\textbf{(M)}

\item
During a sufficiently large excursion, the effective kernel population
decreases.
This is observed directly in the \(T=0.1\) collapse trajectory, and low
\(\neff\) is the strongest prospective within-row predictor of future bursts.
\textbf{(M)}

As the population becomes small, the correction is concentrated among fewer
features, potentially increasing the gain carried by each remaining
nondegenerate direction.
\textbf{(I)}

\item
In the exact single-feature limit,

\[
\neff=1,
\]

the corrected support Jacobian vanishes and

\[
\Pisurr=0.
\]

This limit is observed in one post-collapse block.
At the same time, neighboring blocks with
\(\neff\approx2\) and very large \(z\) can have

\[
\Pisurr
\sim
10^{12}.
\]

Disabling the support gradient stops post-collapse scale growth, showing that
these remaining surrogate contributions are causally important.
\textbf{(M)}

\item
Once several blocks have entered the high-scale regime, scale amplification
propagates through depth in the residual-replacement architecture, as observed
in the earlier investigation.
The trajectory eventually reaches a high-scale state in which most of the
kernel is effectively inactive.
\textbf{(M)}

\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommended controller variables}
% ============================================================

The experiments suggest separating two functions that were previously
combined in the \(\chigeo\)-based controller.

\subsection*{Fast warning variable: effective kernel population}

The strongest prospective within-row warning signal is the effective
population

\[
\neff.
\]

Low \(\neff\) reaches within-row AUC approximately \(0.75\), and population
collapse is directly associated with entry into the near-degenerate regime.

The existing temperature controller already contains a related population
floor based on the number of candidates occupying the kernel window.
The present results support keeping such a fast population-based guard.

However, the intervention should depend on the stage of the instability.

Before collapse, broadening the kernel can restore a healthy distributed
correction.

After a block has already entered the extremely high-scale, near-degenerate
regime, increasing \(T\) can repopulate the kernel while \(z\) is enormous,
producing very large surrogate gain.
The post-collapse intervention shows that this can accelerate rather than
suppress the runaway.

At that stage, the measured safe emergency action is to disable or strongly
reduce the support-gradient contribution until the scale has recovered.

\subsection*{Pressure variable: exact surrogate gain}

The natural measure of support-surrogate strength is

\[
\Pisurr
=
\|L_\kappa D_z\|_F^2.
\]

It is dimensionless, invariant to score-unit reparameterization, and closely
tracks the realized support-gradient gain.

When the exact candidate tensors are unavailable, the forward-only
approximation

\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{approx}}
=
\frac{b^2}{4T\delta}
\]

provides a useful coarse proxy, although it becomes unreliable near
degenerate score geometries.

A controller based directly on \(\Pisurr\) would therefore be a more
principled replacement for the current \(\chigeo\)-based pressure trim.

The existing \(\chigeo\) controller works in the tested regime because
\(\chigeo\) co-varies with surrogate gain across configurations, but
\(\chigeo\) itself has no invariant interpretation and should be treated as a
configuration-level heuristic.

\subsection*{Direct dynamical indicator}

The quantity most directly related to imminent scale instability is not
\(\Pisurr\) or \(\neff\) alone, but the competition between heating and
restoration in the most affected block.

The antithetic one-step probe measures both terms directly.

A useful diagnostic ratio is schematically

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{heat}}
=
\frac{
\text{second-order heating}
}{
|\text{restoring signed drift}|
}.
\]

The measured values are approximately

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{heat}}
\approx
0.02
\]

at calm states,

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{heat}}
\approx
0.7\text{--}1.8
\]

at states whose runs collapse within approximately 2000 steps, and

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{heat}}
\sim
2
\]

roughly 100 steps before collapse.

Thus the direct dynamical criterion suggested by the measurements is

\[
\boxed{
\text{heating becomes comparable to or larger than restoration}.
}
\]

The practical forward-only indicators are the pair

\[
(\Pisurr,\neff)
\]

evaluated per block.

Here \(\Pisurr\) measures the squared gain of the surrogate Jacobian.
At fixed upstream-gradient scale and optimizer response, a larger
\(\Pisurr\) therefore tends to increase the squared parameter-step magnitude,
which is the quantity relevant to second-order heating.

This point is worth stating carefully:
heating is quadratic in the \emph{parameter-step amplitude}.
Because \(\Pisurr\) is already a squared gain, one should not generally expect
heating to scale as \(\Pisurr^2\).
Under otherwise fixed conditions, a roughly linear relation between heating
and \(\Pisurr\) is the more natural leading expectation.

The second quantity, \(\neff\), measures how close the kernel is to the
near-degenerate regime where the distributed correction is lost.

Together these variables provide a mechanistically clearer description than
\(\chigeo\):

\[
\boxed{
\Pisurr
\;\text{measures support-surrogate gain},
\qquad
\neff
\;\text{measures proximity to kernel degeneracy}.
}
\]

\section{Remaining uncertainties and next experiments}
% ============================================================

Several important questions remain open.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item
The destabilizing heating contribution has been identified locally, but the
transfer of scale between successive blocks has not yet been measured
directly.
A block-resolved intervention measuring how a scale perturbation in one block
changes the next block within the same optimizer step would test the proposed
depth-propagation mechanism.

\item
The present drift/heating decomposition is based primarily on one-step
counterfactuals.
These are not identical to sustained closed-loop dynamics because Adam's
second-moment adaptation changes the effective response to repeated large
gradients.
Matched 10--50 step branch continuations from the same checkpoints would
measure this attenuation directly.

\item
The within-row prospective comparison currently contains only three
\((K,T)\) rows with both outcomes.
The interrupted boundary-bracketing campaign, covering sixteen additional
configurations, would substantially strengthen the comparison between
\(\Pisurr\), \(\neff\), and other candidate warning variables.

\item
A controller targeting the exact \(\Pisurr\) has not yet been tested.
The present analysis predicts that such a controller should be more portable
than the \(\chigeo\)-based controller under changes of score units and
parameterization.
This remains an experimental prediction.

\item
The metastable-escape interpretation remains qualitative.
A quantitative escape parameter would require matched estimates of the
restoring slope, excitation strength, and effective collapse boundary,
for example quantities analogous to

\[
(\lambda,D,x_c),
\]

across a larger number of marginal configurations.

\end{itemize}
